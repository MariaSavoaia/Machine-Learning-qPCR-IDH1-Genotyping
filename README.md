# IDH1 qPCR Genotyping: Machine Learning on a Calibrated PCR Model

This project aims to mathematically model the mechanisms underlying allele-specific qPCR genotyping, generate synthetic datasets from this model by stochastic simulation, and then test how different machine learning algorithms approach the data in order to genotype the IDH1 R132H mutation (mutant or wt).

The mathematical model of the PCR reactions includes the variant allele frequency (VAF), the amplification efficiency and various sources of noise during the assay.
On the data generated by this model, we compare unsupervised methods (Gaussian Mixture Models and K-means clustering), as well as supervised ones (logistic regression) in trying to establish decision boundaries between the two clusters of samples (mut and wt), we use a Random Forest regressor to model the relationship between VAF and the amplification signal, and we use PCA to explore the structure of the data. The three classifiers are also compared with fixed absolute Ct thresholds, sometimes used in practice.

## Key findings

All results are for the simulated model described below (10% prevalence, 5,000 fragments).

* **Delta_ct beats absolute Ct cutoffs.** An unsupervised Gaussian Mixture Model on delta_ct reaches 99.3% accuracy and a supervised logistic regression 99.5%, against 89.3% for fixed absolute Ct thresholds. The thresholds fail because they ignore Ct_wt, which makes about 11% of wild-type fragments look mutant.
* **The GMM matches a supervised model without seeing any labels,** but only while the mutation is not too rare. At 5% prevalence and below, a plain GMM latches onto the tail of the wild-type cluster (accuracy drops to 0.73 at 1%), while a GMM initialized with the calibrated cluster means and the prevalence stays within 0.003 of the supervised benchmark in accuracy (although at 1% its PPV is lower, 0.75 against 0.91).
* **K-means is clearly worse (94.2%)** because it assumes clusters of similar size and spread, and the wild-type cluster is wider and about nine times larger.
* **The best decision boundary depends on the prevalence.** It moves from delta_ct ≈ 6.7 at 50% prevalence to ≈ 4.5 at 1%, with sensitivity falling from 0.997 to 0.93, so thresholds set on a cohort with a skewed prevalence do not transfer to the general population.
* **The detection limit is set by the assay's background, not by the amount of DNA.** A single reaction reaches 95% sensitivity only at VAF ≈ 0.2, more than 300 times above the physical limit (VAF ≈ 0.0006, where there are only three to four mutant molecules in the tube).

The real patient cohort (Project 1) is small, and the prevalence of the mutation in the glioma samples tested is skewed compared to the real-world prevalence. Therefore it cannot be used by itself to measure the algorithms’ performance, or to set robust decision thresholds that would hold in a general population. We used it to calibrate most of the parameters of the model above, and this repository runs on synthetic samples, generated to complement the real data. Because their true genotype and VAF are known, we can measure accuracy, sensitivity and detection limits, and we can simulate the decision boundary for different parameter sets (for example at the real-world prevalence of the mutation, set by default at 10%).

Every fragment is simultaneously run in two qPCR reactions: a mutant-specific assay, which amplifies the R132H allele, and a wild-type assay, which amplifies the normal allele. Since the maximum VAF of a heterozygous mutation is 0.5, every fragment carries wild-type alleles, so every fragment must amplify in the wild-type assay. This makes the wild-type reaction a built-in control for DNA quantity and quality: if it fails (Ct_wt ≥ 38), the fragment cannot be genotyped. The genotype is read from the difference between the two threshold cycles, delta_ct = Ct_mut − Ct_wt. A fragment carrying the mutation amplifies early in the mutant assay, so its delta_ct is low, while a wild-type fragment only produces late, non-specific background amplification, so its delta_ct is high. Using delta_ct to classify a fragment as wild-type or mutant is an upgrade from applying absolute Ct cutoffs (which call a fragment wild-type when the mutant assay is late or absent, and mutant when both assays amplify), because calculating delta_ct cancels out (normalizes) the many sources of variation that both individual reactions are often subject to (DNA quantity and quality, technical changes that affect amplification efficiency, etc.), making establishing reliable reproducible absolute cutoffs very difficult. The comparison at the end of this project shows the size of this effect.

## Modeling the PCR reactions

The model reproduces the mechanism that produces these Ct values, simulating one fragment at a time, in four steps: the sample, the two main sources of signal (mutant-specific and background), and how the Ct and delta_ct values are determined. The synthetic data is generated by Monte Carlo sampling from the distributions of the model, and several analyses (the Poisson limit, the prevalence analysis and the robustness checks) estimate their results from many simulated or resampled datasets.

**1. The sample: genotype, variant allele frequency and DNA concentration.** A fragment is wild-type with probability 1 − prevalence (VAF = 0). Otherwise it is mutant, and its VAF is drawn from a Beta(6, 2) distribution scaled to the maximum VAF of 0.5, which is the value for a heterozygous mutation. This skews the mutant samples toward full heterozygosity and leaves a tail of low-VAF samples, as in the real mutant fragments. The DNA concentration is drawn log-uniformly between 13 and 412 ng/µL, the range seen in the real samples.

**2. The mutant-specific signal.** In each cycle, PCR multiplies the amount of amplicon by (1 + E), where E is the efficiency (between 0 and 1). The mutant assay can only amplify mutant copies, which make up a fraction VAF of the template, while the wild-type assay amplifies the rest, 1 − VAF; so a sample with a given VAF reaches the threshold in the mutant assay with a delay of ln(1/VAF - 1) / ln(1 + E) cycles relative to a heterozygous sample (VAF = 0.5). A reaction crosses the detection threshold when its amplicon reaches a fixed amount, so the number of cycles needed is lower when there is more template, and the gap between the two assays is:

$$\Delta Ct_{signal} = \Delta Ct_{pure} - \frac{\operatorname{logit}(VAF)}{\ln(1+E)} + \varepsilon_{signal}, \qquad \varepsilon_{signal} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{signal}^2)$$

where $\operatorname{logit}(VAF) = \ln\frac{VAF}{1-VAF}$. For a heterozygous sample (VAF = 0.5) the logit is 0, so $\Delta Ct_{pure}$ is the delta_ct of a pure heterozygous mutant. The slope of delta_ct against logit(VAF) is therefore −1/ln(1 + E) ≈ −1.54 for E = 92%. Assumptions: both assays have the same, constant efficiency in every cycle and sample, and the noise is Gaussian and independent of VAF.

**3. The background signal.** Wild-type samples are not “empty” in the mutant assay: it produces late, non-specific amplification, which we model as a background level that also bounds the signal of low-VAF mutants. We found in the real wild-type samples that this level depends linearly on the logarithm of the DNA concentration:

$$\Delta Ct_{background} = \mu_{bg} + \beta\,(\log_{10} c - \log_{10} c_{ref}) + \varepsilon_{bg}, \qquad \varepsilon_{bg} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{bg}^2)$$

Here $\mu_{bg}$ is the mean background and $c_{ref}$ is the concentration where the background equals this mean. Biologically speaking, the initial DNA concentration shouldn’t have an effect on the assays because all original samples were diluted to the same concentration of DNA, however, this is the empirical result of a regression from the real data, and we treat the background as non-specific amplification, not as a specific mechanism.

**4. What the reaction reports.** The mutant reaction crosses the threshold at whichever amplification arrives first, the true mutant signal or the background:

$$\Delta Ct = \min(\Delta Ct_{signal},\ \Delta Ct_{background})$$

Wild-type samples have no mutant-specific signal ($\Delta Ct_{signal} = +\infty$), so they always report the background. A mutant with a high VAF is dominated by the signal, and a mutant with a very low VAF cannot be told apart from the background, which is what creates the detection limit. The Ct values are then built as $Ct_{wt} = 25 + \varepsilon_{quality}$ (an arbitrary reference baseline plus sample quality noise, independent of concentration) and $Ct_{mut} = Ct_{wt} + \Delta Ct$. We simulate $Ct_{wt}$ independently of VAF, so the whole effect of VAF is carried by delta_ct. A reaction that has not crossed the threshold within the 50 cycles of a run is right-censored: its Ct is set to 50 and flagged as undetermined, instead of being dropped.

**Where the parameters come from.** E, the Beta shape and the Ct_wt baseline are assumptions, the prevalence is a simulation choice, and the remaining parameters are calibrated on the real data.

| Parameter | Default | How it was obtained |
|---|---|---|
| Prevalence | 0.10 | Set by us (a simulation choice) |
| Maximum VAF | 0.5 | Heterozygous mutation |
| E (efficiency) | 0.92 (ln(1 + E) ≈ 0.65) | Assumed, within the 90–100% range expected for a working assay |
| VAF distribution | Beta(6, 2) × 0.5 | Assumed, to match the spread of the real mutant fragments |
| $\Delta Ct_{pure}$ | 1.14 | Mean of the mutant GMM cluster in the real data |
| $\sigma_{signal}$ | 1.66 | Standard deviation of the mutant GMM cluster |
| $\mu_{bg}$ | 15.25 | Mean of the wild-type GMM cluster |
| $\beta$ | −3.86 | Slope of the regression of wild-type delta_ct on log10(concentration) |
| $\sigma_{bg}$ | 3.02 | Residual standard deviation of that regression |
| $c_{ref}$ | 67 ng/µL | Concentration at which the regression equals $\mu_{bg}$ |
| Concentration range | 13–412 ng/µL | Observed range, drawn log-uniformly |
| $Ct_{wt}$ baseline, $\sigma_{quality}$ | 25, 3.62 | Arbitrary reference point; residual standard deviation of Ct_wt in the real data |
| Maximum cycles | 50 | Length of one run |

Fragments are also analyzed with a dilution-series-style dataset: a fixed grid of known VAFs from 1e-5 to 0.5, with 50 replicates each. This is used for the regression and the sensitivity analysis. The Poisson sampling of mutant molecules is applied only in the physical-limit analysis, not in the main dataset.

## Analyses

### 1. Quality control and calibration checks

Before any model is fitted, the synthetic population (5,000 fragments, about 10% mutant) is checked against the real data. The delta_ct histogram, split by true genotype, shows the two expected clusters, and we compare their mean and spread with the GMM parameters measured on the real cohort. The mutant cluster (taken from fragments with VAF close to the maximum, where the signal channel should match the real mutant cluster) and the mean of the wild-type cluster are close to the real values. The one visible difference is the spread of the wild-type cluster, which is about 7% wider in the simulation than in the real data, whichever way the spread is measured (the SD of the fitted wild-type GMM component is 3.31 against 3.09 in the real cohort, and the SD of the true wild-type fragments is 3.37 against 3.14). The reason is the concentration distribution: the real wild-type samples have a narrower spread of log10 concentration (SD 0.26) than the log-uniform distribution of the generator (SD 0.43), and the concentration slope turns that spread into extra delta_ct variance. With the real spread, the model predicts a wild-type SD of 3.19, close to the 3.14 measured directly on the real samples, so the calibration itself is consistent. We kept the log-uniform distribution over the observed range and treat the difference as a known limitation.

Two kinds of failed reactions are handled explicitly. Technical failures are fragments whose wild-type reaction did not amplify properly (Ct_wt ≥ 38, the cutoff used on the real data): since every fragment carries wild-type alleles, these cannot be genotyped. They are very rare in the synthetic data (2 of 5,000). Right-censored reactions are those that never crossed the threshold within the 50 cycles of a run, which is more common (about 2% of fragments, all in the mutant assay). Dropping them would remove exactly the wild-type fragments with the lowest background, so in the main population they are kept, with their Ct set to 50 and flagged as undetermined. The dilution-series dataset and the datasets generated for the prevalence analysis drop them instead (which is why the specificity below rests on 48 of 50 wild-type reactions). These reactions would be called wild-type anyway, so dropping them only very slightly raises the sensitivity at the lowest VAFs (7 of the 2,550 dilution-series reactions are dropped).

### 2. Linear regression of delta_ct on VAF

To check that the simulated signal behaves as the model predicts, we use the dilution-series-style dataset: a fixed grid of known VAFs, spaced logarithmically from 1e-5 to 0.5 with 50 replicates each, plus a wild-type reference point. The model says that delta_ct is linear in logit(VAF), with a slope of −1/ln(1 + E) ≈ −1.54 and an intercept equal to the pure-mutant delta_ct, so we fit this line to the mutant reactions in two ways. Over the full VAF range, the slope is flatter than the theory, and the intercept is higher. This is the effect of the background: at low VAF the mutant signal arrives later than the background, the observed delta_ct is the minimum of the two, and the curve flattens toward the background level instead of continuing along the line. Restricting the fit to VAF > 0.01, where the signal dominates, brings the slope and the intercept close to the theoretical values, which confirms that the simulation reproduces the equation it was built on. The R² is lower in the restricted fit only because the range of logit(VAF) is smaller while the replicate noise stays the same. The plot shows the mean ± SD of delta_ct at every VAF, the two fits and the wild-type background level.

### 3. Random Forest regression

The logit-linear model is one specific functional form. To check whether a flexible model finds structure that it misses, we fit a Random Forest regressor that predicts delta_ct from log10(VAF), using the mutant reactions of the same dataset and a 70/30 train/test split. On the held-out data, the Random Forest explains about as much variance as the logit-linear fit (R² of 0.81 against 0.80), so it is not meaningfully better, and its error (about 2 cycles) is close to the replicate-to-replicate noise of the assay. Most of the remaining error is therefore noise that no model can remove, and the logit-linear relationship already captures the shape of the curve. Two caveats apply: the linear fit is evaluated on all the data while the forest is evaluated on a held-out set, which slightly favors the linear model, and a forest cannot extrapolate beyond the VAF range it was trained on, while the logit-linear model can.

### 4. PCA

PCA is unsupervised, so it shows what structure exists in the data before any classifier is used. The three input variables are standardized: ct_mut, log10(DNA concentration), and the Ct_wt residual (the part of Ct_wt not explained by concentration, used as a proxy for sample quality). By design, and following what was observed in the real data, Ct_wt does not depend on concentration (R² = 0.0001 in the synthetic data), so the concentration effect in the mutant channel appears only in ct_mut. The three components explain 54%, 33% and 13% of the variance. Because the residual is uncorrelated with concentration by definition, these proportions are fixed by how ct_mut correlates with the other two variables, so they reflect the choice of inputs and not new structure in the data.

The loadings show how the variation is organized. PC2 is the concentration axis (loading 0.92 on log10 concentration and 0 on ct_mut). PC1 and PC3 both contain ct_mut, with the quality residual added in PC1 and subtracted in PC3. The plots, colored by the true genotype, show where the information is:

* **PC1 vs PC2:** the mutants are shifted toward negative PC1, but they overlap heavily with the wild-type samples, so neither component separates the genotypes.
* **PC1 vs PC3 and PC2 vs PC3:** the mutants (low PC3) and the wild-type samples (high PC3) form two nearly separate groups, so the genotype is carried by PC3. Since PC3 subtracts the quality residual from ct_mut, it removes the Ct_wt variation that both channels share, which is the same idea as using delta_ct instead of ct_mut alone. PC2, the concentration axis, adds no separation.

### 5. Gaussian Mixture Model (unsupervised)

**Fit and boundary.** A two-component GMM is fitted on delta_ct without using the genotype labels. Each component is a normal distribution with its own mean, variance and weight, fitted by maximum likelihood, and the component with the lower mean is called the mutant cluster. The fit recovers the two populations, with weights close to the simulated prevalence (about 10% mutant), and the BIC score is lowest for two components, which supports the two-genotype structure. The decision boundary is the delta_ct where the weighted densities of the two components are equal, so that P(Mut | delta_ct) = 0.5:

$$\pi_{mut}\,\mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu_{mut}, \sigma_{mut}^2) = \pi_{wt}\,\mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu_{wt}, \sigma_{wt}^2)$$

It falls at delta_ct ≈ 6.1. Since the variances differ, it is not the midpoint of the two means, and since it uses the weights, it moves with the prevalence. Against the true genotype, the GMM reaches an accuracy of 99.3%, even though it never sees the labels. The fitted mutant mean is higher than that of the near-maximum-VAF fragments in the calibration check, because the fitted component describes the whole mutant population, including lower-VAF fragments with a later signal.

**Prevalence.** The prior probability of the mutation changes where the boundary should be, and how reliable a positive call is. For each prevalence from 50% down to 1%, we generate a new training population and an independent test population at that prevalence. As a supervised benchmark, each class gets its own normal density fitted on the true labels, with the prior stated explicitly. At lower prevalence the optimal boundary moves toward lower delta_ct, because a mutant call needs stronger evidence when mutants are rare. The price is a lower sensitivity (from 0.997 at 50% to 0.93 at 1%), while the specificity stays above 0.99, so the PPV remains above 0.9 even at 1%. At 10% prevalence this benchmark puts the boundary at delta_ct ≈ 5.7, slightly below the full-data GMM (6.1) and slightly above the logistic regression (about 5.6). The three values differ because the methods make different assumptions: the benchmark fits one normal density per true class on a population from which the censored reactions were dropped, while the GMM is fitted without labels on all fragments, including the censored ones, and its mutant component also absorbs the low-VAF mutants.

**Unsupervised GMM versus the supervised benchmark.** The unsupervised GMM has to find the mutant cluster on its own and learns the mutant weight from the data. We compare a plain GMM (default start), a GMM initialized with the real-data cluster means and the prevalence as starting weights, and the supervised benchmark, on sensitivity, specificity, and mut cluster mean on an independent test set. The plain GMM matches the benchmark down to about 10% prevalence, then fails: at 5% prevalence and below, its lower component latches onto the left tail of the wild-type cluster instead of the mutant group, and its accuracy drops to 0.73 at 1%. The initialized GMM stays within 0.003 of the benchmark in accuracy at every prevalence. Accuracy hides a difference at low prevalence, however, because almost all fragments are wild-type. The mutant component of the initialized GMM is broader and heavier than the true mutant cluster (at 1% prevalence its weight is 0.022 against a true 0.01, and its mean 4.2 against 2.1), so its boundary sits higher: it is more sensitive than the benchmark (0.98 against 0.93 at 1%) but less specific (0.997 against 0.999), and since mutants are rare, these extra false positives lower its PPV to 0.75 against 0.91. The unsupervised GMM is therefore reliable only while its mutant mean stays near the true mutant cluster, and at low prevalence it needs an informed starting point.

**Calibration.** The GMM gives each fragment a posterior probability of being mutant,

$$P(Mut \mid x) = \frac{\pi_{mut}\,\mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu_{mut}, \sigma_{mut}^2)}{\pi_{mut}\,\mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu_{mut}, \sigma_{mut}^2) + \pi_{wt}\,\mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu_{wt}, \sigma_{wt}^2)}$$

and we check whether these probabilities match the observed frequencies in 20 equal-count bins. The Brier score is very low (0.0046). Most fragments are far from the boundary and have a posterior of 0 or 1, where the calibration is exact. In the transition region the model slightly overestimates the probability of a mutant, which is expected since the real class distributions are only approximately Gaussian.

**Sensitivity and limit of detection.** The fitted classifier is applied to the dilution-series dataset. The specificity is 1.00, but it rests on only 48 wild-type reactions (the false-positive rate below is estimated on many more). The sensitivity is about 0 below VAF ≈ 0.003, reaches 50-60% around VAF ≈ 0.04 and 95% only at VAF ≈ 0.2, which is the limit of detection (LOD95). The lower the VAF of a mutant, the later its signal appears, so its delta_ct rises as the VAF falls. The classifier calls a fragment mutant when its delta_ct is below the boundary (about 6.1), and the average mutant reaches that value at VAF ≈ 0.04, where about half of the replicates fall below it and are called mutant, and half fall above it. Replicates of the same VAF differ from each other by about 1.7 cycles (the noise of the assay), so for 95% of them to fall below the boundary, the average signal has to sit well under it (by about 1.6 standard deviations, or roughly 2.7 cycles), which only happens at VAF ≈ 0.2. Each point is based on 50 replicates, so the LOD95 is an approximate value.

**Physical sampling limit.** A reaction can only produce a mutant signal if at least one mutant molecule is in the tube. With 20 ng of DNA (6.6 pg per diploid genome, 2 alleles each) there are about 6,061 alleles per reaction, so the number of mutant molecules is Poisson distributed with mean VAF × 6,061, and the probability of at least one reaches 95% at VAF ≈ 0.0006. When the simulated signal is gated by this probability, the classifier’s single-reaction sensitivity reaches 95% only at VAF ≈ 0.2, with a false-positive rate of 0.4% on wild-type reactions. The two limits answer different questions. The physical limit tells us when a mutant molecule is present in the reaction at all, and at VAF ≈ 0.0006 there are already three to four of them on average (the exact 95% point is a mean of three molecules, at VAF ≈ 0.0005; the reported value is the first point of the VAF grid that reaches 95%). The classifier’s limit tells us when that molecule’s signal can be told apart from the background, which every wild-type reaction also produces. At very low VAF, the mutant signal appears only slightly earlier than this background, so the boundary that keeps the false-positive rate on wild-type samples at 0.4% leaves most of these mutants on the wild-type side. This puts the classifier’s limit more than 300 times above the physical limit (the exact ratio depends on the resolution of the VAF grid), so the detection limit of the assay is set by how well the mutant signal separates from the background, not by the amount of mutant DNA in the tube.

**Robustness.** We check how much the boundary depends on the particular sample:

* In 1,000 bootstrap resamples, the GMM refit recovers a minority mutant component in 999, and the 95% interval of the boundary is about 5.8 to 6.5. About 2% of these refits (21) form a separate small group with a boundary around 8.5, where the mutant component absorbs the lower shoulder of the wild-type cluster; the interval describes the main group.
* In stratified 5-fold cross-validation, all the refits are stable, and only 5 fragments change label compared to the full fit, all right next to the boundary.
* In refits on random subsets, the fit becomes stable only with enough fragments: it works in about half of the subsets of 20, three quarters of 100, and nearly all of 1,000 or more. At 10% prevalence, 100 fragments contain only about 10 mutants. The subsets come from the same 5,000 fragments, so the large ones overlap and understate the variability that new datasets would show.

### 6. K-means clustering (unsupervised)

K-means with k = 2 also uses only delta_ct, but it assigns each fragment to the nearest of two centers and moves the centers to minimize the within-cluster squared distance, with the boundary at the midpoint between them. The silhouette score is highest at k = 2, consistent with the two genotypes. The boundary lands at delta_ct ≈ 9.8, much higher than the GMM’s, and the accuracy is 94.2%. K-means finds all the mutants but calls 290 wild-type fragments mutant, so its precision for the mutant class is only 0.63. This follows from its assumptions: clusters of similar size and spread. Our wild-type cluster is much wider and about nine times larger than the mutant cluster, so the midpoint falls inside the lower shoulder of the wild-type cluster, and the mutant center is pulled up as it absorbs that shoulder. The GMM, which fits a separate variance and weight for each component, avoids this.

### 7. Logistic regression (supervised)

Logistic regression is the only supervised method here: it is trained on the true labels, using Ct_wt and Ct_mut as two separate features and not their difference, with a stratified 70/30 train/test split. The two fitted coefficients are almost equal and opposite, so the model learns from the labels alone that the genotype depends on the difference between the two channels. Its boundary, the line where P(Mut) = 0.5, is

$$Ct_{mut} = 5.45 + 1.005 \times Ct_{wt}$$

which is almost exactly a delta_ct threshold of about 5.6 (5.56 to 5.62 over the range of Ct_wt). The held-out accuracy is 99.5%. Because the mutant class is only about 10% of the data, we also report metrics that stay meaningful under class imbalance, using stratified 5-fold cross-validation with out-of-fold predictions (each fragment is predicted by a model that never saw it): the ROC-AUC is 0.9996 and the PR-AUC is 0.996, against a no-skill precision-recall baseline equal to the prevalence (0.10). The PR-AUC is the stricter of the two, because the ROC-AUC does not change with the class balance.

### 8. Decision boundaries compared

The boundaries of the three classifiers are plotted together, in the Ct_wt/Ct_mut plane and on the delta_ct axis, next to the absolute Ct thresholds used in practice (technical failure if Ct_wt ≥ 38, mutant if Ct_mut < 34). The GMM and K-means boundaries are two parallel diagonals, and the logistic regression boundary is almost parallel to them, while the absolute thresholds are a vertical and a horizontal line. On the delta_ct axis, the logistic regression boundary depends slightly on Ct_wt, so it is shown as a band covering the 5th to 95th percentile of Ct_wt.

### 9. Comparison of all methods

The last step puts the three classifiers next to the absolute thresholds, using the true genotype as the reference. The logistic regression (99.5%, 27 errors in 5,000) and the GMM (99.3%, 36 errors) are nearly tied (the logistic regression is scored here on all 5,000 fragments, 70% of which it was trained on; its held-out accuracy on the remaining 30% is also 99.5%, so this does not change the comparison), even though the GMM never sees the labels, K-means is at 94.2% because of the cluster-shape assumption described above, and the absolute thresholds are at 89.3%. The thresholds make 535 errors, mainly by calling wild-type fragments mutant (510 of them; the others are 23 mutants called wild-type and the 2 technical failures, which are wild-type fragments with Ct_wt ≥ 38 that the thresholds refuse to call and which count as errors here): the cutoff Ct_mut < 34 ignores Ct_wt, and in wild-type fragments Ct_mut varies with the whole reaction (mean about 40, SD about 5), so about 11% of them fall below 34 just because the reaction amplified early. Delta_ct removes this shared variation, which the other three methods use or learn. All four methods agree on 4,348 of the 5,000 fragments, and almost all of the remaining disagreements are wild-type fragments that the thresholds call mutant. The GMM and the logistic regression disagree on only 23 fragments, all close to their boundaries.

## Limitations

* **The generator and the methods share the same assumptions.** The data is simulated from a model whose clusters are approximately Gaussian, and several of the methods assume the same, so the results show how each method behaves under this model and not how accurate it would be on new patient samples. Testing that would need real samples with an independent ground truth for the genotype, for example from sequencing.
* **The calibration inherits the limits of the real cohort.** The parameters come from a small cohort whose mutation prevalence is skewed compared to the real-world prevalence, and their uncertainty is not propagated into the simulation. We chose a prevalence of 10% as a simulation setting, and the prevalence analyses show how the results change with this choice.
* **The wild-type spread is overestimated.** The simulated wild-type cluster is about 7% wider than the real one, because the generator draws the DNA concentration log-uniformly over the observed range, which is more spread out than the real wild-type concentrations, so the error rates among wild-type fragments are likely somewhat pessimistic.
* **The dependence of the background on concentration is empirical.** It comes from a regression on the real wild-type samples and has no mechanistic explanation here (see above).
* **The model is a simplification of the assay.** It has no batch or plate effects and no replicate wells, the efficiency is constant over the cycles and the same in both assays, the noise is Gaussian and independent, and only a heterozygous R132H mutation is simulated, with the VAF spread described by an assumed Beta distribution.
* **The detection limits are for one reaction.** The limit of detection is estimated from 50 replicates per VAF point, on a single reaction with 20 ng of input DNA, and replicate reactions would lower it.
* **The headline accuracies come from a single simulated dataset.** The classifiers are compared on one random draw of the model (one seed), so small differences, such as the difference of 9 errors between the GMM (36) and the logistic regression (27), should not be over-interpreted. Repeating the whole pipeline across many seeds would give a spread for each accuracy.
